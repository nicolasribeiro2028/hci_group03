# Interview 1
This person is a Medicine BSc student at ETH, who finds their subjects on average to be a number 4 in the scale of complexity going from 1 being the easiest to 5 being the hardest. He is part of the group of people that go directly looking for help even before having tried to solve it for themselves, saying explicitly that they on average take less than 1 minute of their time to try to solve the problem on their own before looking for help. The types of problems that he has to solve tend to go around memorizing with Anki, and diagnosing virtual patients based on provided information. 

What stood out about this conversation was that they have a very structured way of going about looking for help; He starts by going back to the beginning of the problem to start over, then checking the material provided by the lecturer to know which things to ask, before then going to either friends or directly to AI in the hunt for more meaningful questions that can lead him to properly diagnose the patient. With friends he tends to get answers with a low level of certainty, whereas he completely trusts AI with what it provides. The one situation in which he regarded the help as not useful was when needing to code something. He reached for AI from the beginning and said that the answer provided didn’t work, plus he dint know how to correct the code. In the end he couldn’t submit the homework and was left confused. We also found out that when dealing with tasks where he had struggled in the past, he would not even try and directly jump to AI (Maths and Physics specifically), whereas with tasks that he half understood he would improve and need less and less help as time went on. The level of commitment was key here, with topics where he knew he could do something, he would try his best and ask for indirect help (Friends or even AI), whereas with topics where he struggled he seemed to give up immediately and look for the exact answer (Yet sometimes he would regret it upon closer inspection since he would realize that he could actually solve it for himself). 

# Interview 2
This person is a graduate as of September 2026 from Mechanical Engineering BSc. The courses had an average of 4 on the complexity scale of 1 being the easiest and 5 being the hardest according to him. Generally he had to solve problems that had to do with considering a provided context, and finding out more information using mathematical equations. His approach is almost exclusively to try on his own, taking around 15 minutes of attempts before looking for help. When approaching problems he starts by using intuition and gathering formulas, he tries to combine them in a way that yields the solution. If this doesn’t work he directly goes to AI. Surprisingly he doesn’t go to obtain the complete solution, but rather to check on what he had done so far and where he got stuck, before going again to try on his own. Sometimes he would instead read the solutions up to the point where he got stuck to see the next step or corroborate that the previously done work goes in the correct direction. Generally these two approaches where found to be quite positive, with the only problem being when the help is on a way higher level than where he thinks he is in terms of the topic he is trying to learn. Sometimes he said he “lacked the basics” to even comprehend what the help was trying to hint at. Curiously he blamed himself and not AI for not being up for the task.

After receiving help he starts needing less and less of it as he thinks the “possible cases” are already covered and thus he can refer back to them for similar tasks. The only situations in which he has to go back to the same type of help is when the case was very specific and he hadn’t seen something like it for a while. 

In the end the discussion focused on how to him AI is the way to go, skipping checking slides, looking for other resources and even reaching out to people, as to him they are either to vague, or they directly provide the answer. He thinks the tailored nature of AI is precisely what he needs when he knows what to ask. Interesting quote: “My peers make mistakes, unlike AI, hence I go directly to it”.
