# Interview 1 Summary
TThis participant is a 3rd-year Computer Science BSc student at TU Berlin (5th/6th semester) who rates his coursework difficulty as 4 out of 5. His current workload combines advanced modules like Software Engineering and Distributed Systems (*Softwaretechnik und Verteilte Systeme*), Compiler Construction (*Compilerbau*), and system programming projects, alongside theoretical algorithms. He typically insists on working independently for 15 to 20 minutes before seeking external support, although this patience drops to just a couple of minutes when facing tight assignment deadlines.

He reported a clear contrast in his study methods. For some lectures (more content heavy) he uses AI to get quick, high-level overviews of dense theoretical topics before reading lecture slides alone, but relies on Claude Code in the terminal for programming assignments. In a recent assignment involving C++ programing in a distributed systems task, he turned to the terminal assistant after struggling for 20 minutes. While the tool correctly identified the flawed code and provided the full fix, he reflected that this eliminated the hands-on debugging effort, resulting in an illusion of competence without building long-term memory for implementation details. This usually backfires during written pen-and-paper exams, where he experienced mental blocks on content he had previously outsourced to AI. For him, genuinely helpful support should act like an interactive teaching assistant that pinpoints errors and offers directional hints without writing the final solution.

## Key Findings
- Persists independently for 15–20 minutes, but collapses under deadline stress.
- Receiving complete code solutions creates a false sense of knowledge and harms exam recall.
- AI is effective for conceptual overviews, but counterproductive when completely replacing hands-on coding, if the goal is to learn for the exam.
- Helpful support should provide hints and error diagnostics rather than complete answers.

# Interview 2 Summary

This participant is an Information Systems (*Wirtschaftsinformatik*) BSc student at TU Munich (TUM) who rates his course difficulty as 4 out of 5. His studies balance management concepts with applied data analysis and system modeling. His study routine centers on continuously synthesizing lecture slides into personal Notion summaries. When stuck on a problem, he usually works alone for 10 to 15 minutes, seeking assistance once he enters an unproductive loop or faces impending deadlines.

Unlike students using AI strictly for coding, he uses a permanent ChatGPT Plus tab as a sounding board to validate intermediate reasoning. In a recent modeling assignment, he asked the tool to check his sequence of analytical steps. While his primary goal was validation, deadline pressure also made him welcome having the tool provide a finished four-step structure to speed up completion. The main drawback was a loss of independent confidence in his own answers, because the explanation of the AI seemed immediately intuitive, he adopted it without forming his own arguments, leaving him unable to defend the solution during discussions with peers. He also noted that constant reassurance creates anxiety in unassisted exam environments, where no verification is available. He advocates for support systems that introduce reflective friction—prompting students to rethink assumptions rather than delivering immediate answers.

## Key Findings
- Uses AI primarily as a validation partner to confirm intermediate logic.
- Immediate confirmation creates reliance, eroding confidence during unassisted exams.
- Accepting pre-formulated explanations leads to unearned fluency and difficulty defending work.
- Supportive interfaces should prompt reflection and question assumptions instead of giving immediate solutions.
